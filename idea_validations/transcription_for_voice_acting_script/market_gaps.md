The opportunity is the missing layer between a raw script or audio file and a booth-ready performance document. Generic transcription tools produce words. Voice actors need timed, pronounceable, revision-safe scripts they can perform under deadline. Because no source posts were available, the pain points below are inferred from how commercial, narration, and dubbing work actually runs. Validate them with 10–15 working actors before building.

**Inferred pain points**
- Scripts arrive as messy PDFs, emails, or decks and are unreadable in a booth.
- Generic transcripts drop emphasis, pauses, alt lines, and legal reads.
- Names, brands, and technical terms have no approved pronunciation.
- Auditions and live-directed sessions move faster than manual formatting.
- Revisions multiply versions across actor, director, and client.
- Audiobook and dubbing jobs need continuity (character, timing, lexicon) that one-off tools ignore.

**Executive summary**  
The wedge is not “another transcription app.” It is performance script preparation: turn any source into a cold-readable, timed, pronounced, versioned script, then go deep in one segment (commercial VO, audiobook series, or dubbing). Near-term money is a fast formatter plus a paid human accuracy tier. Longer-term defensibility is the approved lexicon and revision history that travel with a client or a book series. Category dominance is realistic only inside a narrow job type, not against Descript or Rev in general transcription.

### 1. Market Segmentation

**SpotRead — Commercial clock scripts**  
Turns a 15/30/60-second spot into a booth card with runtime estimate, breath marks, legal-line highlighting, and alt takes separated from the hero read.  
Features: spot-length templates, disclaimer callouts, one-page cold-read layout, export to PDF and teleprompter text.  
Value: actors stop losing takes to overtime or buried legal copy.  
Model: per-script fee ($8–25) or a small monthly credit pack for working commercial actors.  
Pain points: messy source files, speed, unreadable formatting.  
Audience: non-union and union commercial voice actors, small VO agencies, ad producers.  
Challenges: runtime estimates will be wrong without a voice profile; legal copy must never be silently rewritten.  
Best-in-category potential: high inside commercial VO. Generic tools do not think in clocks and disclaimers. Narrow scope is the advantage.

**SeriesBible — Audiobook narration continuity**  
Builds a persistent character and pronunciation bible across a book or series, then formats each chapter for long-form reading.  
Features: character voice tags, recurring-name lexicon with client audio clips, chapter chunking, “previously pronounced” flags.  
Value: narrators do not re-research names at chapter 40, and indie authors stop getting inconsistent reads.  
Model: per-title setup fee plus monthly or per-finished-hour subscription while the book is in production.  
Pain points: pronunciation, multi-character clarity, revision chaos over long projects.  
Audience: ACX and indie-audiobook narrators; author-narrators; small audiobook publishers.  
Challenges: long sales cycle per title; lexicon quality depends on client sign-off.  
Best-in-category potential: strong. Stickiness is the bible, not the transcript. A narrator who has recorded three books in a series will not switch mid-series.

**DubCard — Lip-sync and ADR script cards**  
Produces timecoded line cards with constrained length, pause windows, and on-screen speaker labels for dubbing and ADR.  
Features: timecode in/out, line-length warnings, original-language reference beside the read line, take-number field.  
Value: actors and studios stop hand-building cue sheets from subtitles.  
Model: per-minute studio pricing or a seat license for localization houses.  
Pain points: timing, multi-character clarity, version control.  
Audience: dubbing actors, localization studios, game-VO coordinators.  
Challenges: smaller buyer set; needs reliable timecode from source video, which many files lack.  
Best-in-category potential: moderate to high in a niche with real willingness to pay, but sales are relationship-driven and volume is lower.

### 2. Product Differentiation

**BoothCard — Stripped cold-read formatter (low price)**  
One upload, one job: a high-contrast, large-type, one-column script with speaker labels, pause slashes, and alt lines visually demoted. No AI “acting notes.”  
Features: PDF/email/Doc ingest, 30-second turnaround target, print and phone prompter views, zero account required for the first script.  
Value: the audition script is readable before the deadline, not after.  
Model: freemium (watermark or 2 free scripts) then low subscription or pay-per-script.  
Pain points: unreadable formatting, audition speed.  
Audience: beginners through working actors who audition daily.  
Challenges: easy to copy; must be dramatically faster and cleaner than “paste into Google Docs.”  
Best-in-category potential: medium as a product, high as a wedge. Win on speed and readability, then upsell pronunciation and revisions. This is the practical entry product.

**ApprovedRead — Pronunciation-locked premium prep**  
A human editor plus client confirmation produces an approved lexicon (spelling, phonetic spelling, short audio) baked into the script. Nothing ships until the client checks names and legal lines.  
Features: review link for the client, locked glossary, change log, “do not improvise” flags on legal or brand lines.  
Value: the expensive failure mode—mispronounced brand or name on a paid spot—is designed out.  
Model: premium per-project ($75–300 depending on length) with rush fees.  
Pain points: pronunciation, client trust, revisions.  
Audience: brand, medical, financial, and e-learning clients; actors who bill those jobs.  
Challenges: human ops and turnaround SLAs; quality control is the product.  
Best-in-category potential: high on trust, not on technology. “The script the brand already approved” is a defensible claim if the review workflow is strict.

**AltTake — Director-annotated performance script**  
Same words, plus optional emphasis, pace, and alternate readings marked so they cannot be confused with the copy.  
Features: emphasis marks, pause notation, clearly labeled alts, director-comment sidebar that hides in booth view.  
Value: fewer “what did they mean by this line?” emails before the session.  
Model: add-on to BoothCard or a studio seat fee.  
Pain points: missing performance cues, messy notes in margins.  
Audience: directed commercial sessions, e-learning producers, dialogue coaches.  
Challenges: bad annotations are worse than none; keep acting advice optional and visually separate from the copy.  
Best-in-category potential: moderate. Useful, but taste-driven. Do not claim the tool “directs.” Claim it separates direction from copy.

### 3. Business Model Innovation

**CreditDesk — Actor subscription with rollover credits**  
Monthly plan aimed at people who audition several times a week: formatting credits, a shared personal lexicon, and cheap rush. Unused credits roll one month.  
Features: personal name list, template memory (their preferred booth layout), email-in ingest.  
Value: script prep becomes a utility, not a panic task.  
Model: $19–49/month consumer SaaS. Expansion via pronunciation add-on.  
Pain points: speed, repeated pronunciation lookups, formatting.  
Audience: full-time auditioning voice actors.  
Challenges: churn if auditions slow; free tier must not subsidize heavy PDF parsing forever.  
Best-in-category potential: medium. Good business, weak moat unless the personal lexicon and templates are genuinely better than a folder of Word docs.

**PrepBench — Marketplace for overflow and specialty scripts**  
Studios and actors post a script; vetted prep editors claim it against a published SLA. Platform handles payment, glossary lock, and delivery format.  
Features: editor profiles by niche (medical, game, dubbing), fixed-price menu, client approval step.  
Value: buyers get a human when the file is ugly; editors get demand without chasing clients.  
Model: take rate 15–25% on prep fees.  
Pain points: accuracy on hard files, rush capacity, trust.  
Audience: small studios and actors with irregular overflow; freelance script preppers.  
Challenges: chicken-and-egg supply; quality variance; you are in services operations.  
Best-in-category potential: low to medium alone. Better as the human back end of BoothCard than as a standalone marketplace.

**CastingEmbed — White-label prep inside casting tools**  
API or plugin that formats sides the moment a talent is invited to audition, inside tools casting directors already use.  
Features: branded PDF, consistent booth layout, optional lexicon from the brand account, usage analytics for the platform.  
Value: the casting platform looks more professional; actors stop reformatting every side.  
Model: B2B seat or per-audition fee to the platform, not to the actor.  
Pain points: speed, unreadable sides, inconsistent formats across casting sites.  
Audience: casting platforms, talent agencies, in-house brand casting teams.  
Challenges: long enterprise sales; integration work; platforms may build a thin version themselves.  
Best-in-category potential: high if one platform deal lands, fragile if you only have one customer. Pursue after the actor-facing product proves the format.

### 4. Distribution and Marketing

**CoachPipeline — Distribution through VO coaches and demo producers**  
Coaches assign “submit a BoothCard, not a raw PDF” as homework. Demo producers include one formatted script style in the deliverable.  
Features: coach dashboards, student templates, revenue share or free seats for certified coaches.  
Value: new actors learn a professional format; you acquire users at the moment they form habits.  
Model: free for coaches, paid when students convert; or a cohort license.  
Pain points: beginners do not know what a performable script looks like.  
Audience: VO coach alumni, demo-reel clients, workshop students.  
Challenges: coaches protect their brand; revenue share must be simple. Do not imply union or school endorsement you do not have.  
Best-in-category potential: low as a product, high as a channel. This is how a formatter gets its first thousand users without ad spend.

**SessionPartner — Plugin where the session already happens**  
A lightweight companion for remote directed sessions: actor and director see the same script, comments stay off the read view, and the locked file exports at wrap.  
Features: share link, booth view vs. notes view, end-of-session PDF.  
Value: the script stops living in a Zoom chat scroll.  
Model: per-session fee for directors, or included in a studio plan.  
Pain points: revision chaos during live direction, unreadable shared docs.  
Audience: remote session directors, home-studio actors, production coordinators.  
Challenges: you are adjacent to Source-Connect, Zoom, and session platforms; integrate, do not pretend to replace the audio path.  
Best-in-category potential: medium until live sync exists. As a shared, role-based script view it can be the best companion to existing session tools.

**LexiconDrop — Client-owned pronunciation pages as the marketing object**  
Every premium job produces a private link the brand can reuse: “approved names for this campaign.” Actors share it; the next actor on the brand requests the same prep.  
Features: public-to-client glossary page, embeddable audio, “request this lexicon on my next script” button.  
Value: the client asset markets the service without a content blog.  
Model: glossary hosting included in premium prep; paid brand workspace later.  
Pain points: pronunciation inconsistency across a roster of actors.  
Audience: brands with rotating VO talent, e-learning vendors, game publishers.  
Challenges: privacy of unreleased product names; access control matters.  
Best-in-category potential: high as a moat, modest as a first product. The glossary is the asset competitors cannot scrape from a transcript.

### 5. New Paradigm

**PerformScript — Reference-audio to acting script, not a transcript**  
Upload a scratch read or animatic. Output is a script with detected pauses, emphasis, and section timings, clearly marked as inferred, plus a clean copy view with inferences hidden.  
Features: pause and emphasis layer, confidence flags, human override, never overwrites the legal copy.  
Value: directors stop retyping a scratch track; actors see the intended shape of the read.  
Model: usage-based credits on top of the formatter subscription.  
Pain points: missing performance cues, slow manual markup.  
Audience: producers who already record scratch tracks; animation and e-learning teams.  
Challenges: inferred emotion will be wrong often enough to damage trust if you overclaim. Label every inference. Keep the legal words untouched.  
Best-in-category potential: high if accuracy on timing and emphasis is honestly bounded. This is a new category only if you refuse to market it as generic transcription.

**LiveLock — Mid-session revision sync**  
Director edits a line; the actor’s booth view updates with a visible change marker and the previous line still one tap away. At wrap, one canonical file.  
Features: role-based views, change highlighting, offline cache for booth Wi-Fi drops, export of final plus redline.  
Value: remote sessions stop being a race between the actor’s PDF and the director’s latest email.  
Model: studio subscription; per-session overage.  
Pain points: version chaos, live-direction speed.  
Audience: directed remote commercial and game sessions.  
Challenges: reliability bar is brutal; a failed sync during a paid session ends the relationship. Build this after the static formatter is trusted.  
Best-in-category potential: the strongest “only product that does this” claim on the list, and the hardest to ship. Category dominance is possible and so is a reputation hit.

**AccessRead — One source, two deliverables**  
From the same timed script, export a performance booth card and a plain accessibility deliverable (captions or audio-description copy) without mixing stage direction into the spoken words.  
Features: spoken-text vs. direction layers, caption line breaks, AD timing notes kept out of the actor view.  
Value: producers stop paying twice to prepare essentially the same words.  
Model: per-project dual export, sold to producers rather than actors.  
Pain points: timing, format mismatch, copy integrity.  
Audience: e-learning, corporate video, and public-media producers who already owe captions.  
Challenges: accessibility formats have real specs; do not ship a sloppy caption file and call it compliant. Partner or certify before claiming compliance.  
Best-in-category potential: moderate. Real buyer pain, but the buyer is a producer, and the quality bar is regulatory as much as creative.

### Opportunity assessment

| Rank | Solution | Market and growth | Advantage sustainability | Feasibility | Category dominance |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | BoothCard, then ApprovedRead | Broad actor demand; commercial and audition volume is steady | Weak alone; strong once personal and client lexicons attach | High. Narrow scope, clear output, can start semi-manual | Best cold-read formatter for VO, not best transcription tool |
| 2 | SeriesBible | Smaller than commercials, growing with indie audio; high retention per series | Strong. The bible is switching cost | Medium. Needs client sign-off workflow and long-file handling | Best continuity system for series narration |
| 3 | LiveLock | Valuable but limited to directed sessions | Strong if reliability is proven | Low near-term. Session failure is unacceptable | Best shot at a new category, later |

**How to sequence**  
Ship BoothCard as a paid, ugly-PDF-in, booth-card-out service, with humans in the loop where parsing fails. Add ApprovedRead as the premium tier so trust and pronunciation are monetized before you automate them. Use coach distribution for the first users. Build SeriesBible next if audiobook customers show up in the data; build LiveLock only after people already trust your file as the canonical script. Skip a general “AI transcription for voice actors” positioning. That fight is already lost to horizontal tools. The open position is the performable, approved, versioned script.
